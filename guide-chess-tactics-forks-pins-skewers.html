♟️ Chess tactics: forks, pins and skewers explained

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

A tactic is a short sequence of moves that wins material or delivers checkmate, usually because one move creates two threats at once. Strategy tells you where to put your pieces; tactics are how games are actually won. The three patterns below appear in almost every game.

The fork: one piece, two targets

A fork happens when one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time. The opponent can usually save only one of them. Every piece can fork, but the knight is the champion: because it jumps in an L-shape, it often attacks pieces that cannot attack it back. A knight landing on a square where it checks the king and attacks the queen at the same time – a "royal fork" – is one of the most satisfying moves in chess.

Pawns fork too. A pawn pushed between two enemy pieces attacks both, and since a pawn is worth less than any piece, the opponent loses material either way.

How to spot forks: look for enemy pieces that are a knight's jump apart, or on the same line, and ask whether one of your pieces could reach a square that attacks both.

The pin: a piece that cannot move

A pin is when a long-range piece – a bishop, rook or queen – attacks an enemy piece that cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it. If the piece behind is the king, the pin is "absolute": the pinned piece is not allowed to move at all. If the piece behind is the queen or a rook, the pin is "relative": moving is legal but costly.

Pinned pieces are weak. They cannot defend other pieces properly, and you can often attack them again with a pawn. A classic example is a bishop on g5 pinning a knight on f6 against the queen on d8.

The skewer: the pin turned around

A skewer is like a pin, but the more valuable piece is in front. When it moves out of the attack, the piece behind it is captured. The most common skewer is a check along a rank, file or diagonal: the king must step away, and the queen or rook behind it falls.

Other patterns worth knowing

  • Discovered attack – moving one piece uncovers an attack by another piece behind it. If the moving piece also makes a threat, the opponent faces two threats at once.
  • Double check – a discovered attack where both pieces give check. The king must move, because no single move can block or capture both attackers.
  • Removing the defender – capture or chase away the piece that protects something important.
  • Back-rank mate – a king trapped behind its own pawns can be mated by a rook or queen on the last row. Making a small "escape square" for the king (for example by playing h3) prevents it.

A simple checklist before every move

Strong players use a quick routine called "checks, captures and threats":

  1. Look at every check you can give.
  2. Look at every capture you can make.
  3. Look at every threat you can create.

Then do the same from your opponent's point of view. This simple habit finds most tactics, and it stops you from walking into them.

Training your tactical eye

Tactics are pattern recognition. The more you see them, the faster you notice them in your own games. Solve a few puzzles every day, and when you play against the computer in AG Classic Games, pause before each move and run through the checklist above. After a few weeks, forks and pins will start to jump out at you.
