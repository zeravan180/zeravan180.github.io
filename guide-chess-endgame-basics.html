♟️ Chess endgame basics: how to finish the game

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

The endgame is the final stage of a chess game, when most pieces have been exchanged. It is often where games are really decided: a single extra pawn can be enough to win, but only if you know how to use it. Here are the ideas every player should know.

The king becomes a fighting piece

In the opening and middle game the king hides behind its pawns. In the endgame, with fewer attackers left, it is safe to bring the king forward – and you must. An active king in the centre protects its own pawns, attacks the opponent's pawns and helps a passed pawn to promote. A useful habit: as soon as the queens are exchanged, start walking your king towards the middle of the board.

Passed pawns must be pushed

A passed pawn is a pawn with no enemy pawns in front of it or on the neighbouring files, so nothing can stop it except pieces. In the endgame, a passed pawn is often worth more than a minor piece because it forces the opponent to spend time and pieces stopping it. The old advice is "passed pawns must be pushed" – but push them with support, so that the pawn does not simply get captured.

The "rule of the square" helps you know whether a king can catch a running pawn: draw an imaginary square from the pawn to its promotion row. If the defending king can step into that square, it will catch the pawn; if not, the pawn queens.

The opposition

In king and pawn endings, the kings often face each other with one square between them. The player who does not have to move is said to "have the opposition", and that is usually an advantage: the other king must step aside and let the opponent's king through. Learning the opposition is the key to the most common ending of all, king and pawn against king.

For example, with a white king on e6, a white pawn on e5 and a black king on e8, the result depends on who moves. If Black is to move, the black king must give way and White wins; if White is to move, Black can hold the draw. Small details like this decide many real games.

The basic checkmates

Every player should be able to checkmate with:

  • King and queen against king – use the queen to shrink the space of the enemy king, a "knight's move" away from it, then bring your own king up to deliver mate on the edge. Be careful not to stalemate: always leave the enemy king at least one legal move until the final check.
  • King and rook against king – push the enemy king to the edge one row at a time, using your king to take the opposition and the rook to cut off rows or columns.
  • King and two rooks – the "ladder" or "lawnmower" mate: the rooks take turns checking and cutting off rows until the king reaches the edge.

Checkmate with king, bishop and knight is also possible, but it is difficult and rare; it is a good challenge once you know the others.

Rook endings are the most common

Rook endings appear so often that the saying goes "all rook endings are drawn". That is an exaggeration, but it reminds you that an extra pawn in a rook ending is not always enough. Two practical rules:

  • Put your rook behind passed pawns – behind your own pawn to push it, or behind the opponent's pawn to stop it.
  • Keep your rook active. A passive rook tied to defending a pawn often loses even when material is equal.

Stalemate: the last trick of the defender

If the side to move has no legal move and is not in check, the game is a draw by stalemate. When you are winning, always check that your move leaves the opponent at least one legal move. When you are losing, look for stalemate tricks – they save more games than you would think.

How to practise

Set up simple endings against the computer and try to win them: king and queen against king, king and rook against king, and a king and pawn against a lone king. Once you can win these quickly and without stalemate, you will convert many more of your good positions into victories.
