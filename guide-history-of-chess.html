📜 The history of chess: from India to Persia, Baghdad and the world

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

Chess is one of the oldest games still played today, and its journey across the world mirrors the great trade and cultural routes of history. Our region played an important part in that story.

Chaturanga in India

Most historians trace chess back to chaturanga, a game played in northern India by around the sixth century. The name means "four divisions", referring to the four branches of an ancient army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. These became the ancestors of today's pawns, knights, bishops and rooks. Like modern chess, chaturanga was played on an 8×8 board, and the most important piece was the king.

Shatranj in Persia

From India the game travelled to Sasanian Persia, where it became known as chatrang and later shatranj. Persian literature contains some of the earliest written references to the game, and several chess words we still use come from Persian. "Shah" means king, and the phrase shāh māt – often explained as "the king is helpless" – is the origin of the word "checkmate".

The golden age in Baghdad

After the Islamic conquest of Persia, shatranj spread quickly through the Islamic world. Under the Abbasid caliphs, Baghdad became a centre of chess. Players such as al-Adli, al-Suli and al-Lajlaj were famous masters, and they wrote some of the first books on chess strategy and problems. Al-Suli, who lived in the ninth and tenth centuries, was so strong that "playing like al-Suli" became a phrase of praise. Many of their endgame studies, called mansubat, are still admired today.

Shatranj was slower than modern chess. The piece that became the queen, the firzan or vizier, could move only one square diagonally, and the alfil (elephant), ancestor of the bishop, jumped exactly two squares diagonally. Games were long and strategic, and winning often meant capturing all of the opponent's pieces.

Into Europe

Through Muslim Spain (al-Andalus), Sicily and the trade routes of the Mediterranean, chess reached Europe around the tenth century. It became popular among nobles and was used in poems and stories as a symbol of courtly life and of war.

The modern rules

At the end of the fifteenth century, probably in Spain or Italy, the rules changed dramatically. The queen became the most powerful piece on the board, able to move any distance in any direction, and the bishop gained its long diagonal move. This "new chess" was much faster and more aggressive, and it quickly replaced the old game. Castling and the en passant capture were added and standardised over the following centuries.

Chess today

Official world championships began in 1886, and today the international federation FIDE organises tournaments and ratings for players all over the world. Computers, which were once beginners, now play far better than any human, and online play has made it possible to find an opponent at any hour.

When you sit down to play chess – on a board in a café, or on your phone – you are taking part in a tradition that has passed through India, Persia, Baghdad and Europe for some fifteen centuries.
