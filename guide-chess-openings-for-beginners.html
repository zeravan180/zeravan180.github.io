♟️ Chess openings for beginners: five ideas that win more games

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

Most beginners lose their games in the first ten or fifteen moves, long before any clever combination appears on the board. The good news is that you do not need to memorise long lines of opening theory to avoid this. Strong players rely on a handful of ideas, and once you understand them you can play a sensible opening in any position.

1. Fight for the centre

The four squares in the middle of the board – e4, d4, e5 and d5 – are the most valuable real estate in chess. A piece in the centre controls more squares and can move quickly to either side of the board. That is why the most popular first moves are 1.e4 and 1.d4: each pawn occupies one central square and attacks another.

You can also control the centre from a distance. A knight on f3 or c3, or a bishop on a long diagonal, puts pressure on the central squares without standing on them. Either way, the goal is the same: make the middle of the board yours, so that your pieces have room and your opponent's pieces do not.

2. Develop your pieces – knights first

"Development" means bringing your pieces from their starting squares into play. At the start, only the knights can jump over the pawns, so they are usually the first to come out. A good rule of thumb is knights before bishops: the best square for a knight (f3, c3, f6 or c6) is clear from the beginning, while the best square for a bishop often depends on how the pawns are placed.

Try to move each piece only once in the opening. If you move the same knight three times while your opponent brings out three different pieces, you are effectively two moves behind. In chess, time matters as much as material.

3. Do not bring the queen out too early

The queen is your strongest piece, which is exactly why it should usually wait. If it comes out early, your opponent can attack it with knights, bishops and pawns, developing their pieces with every threat while you waste moves running away. A famous example is the "Scholar's Mate" attack with the queen and bishop on f7. It can win against complete beginners, but against anyone who knows to defend with a knight or pawn, the white queen ends up chased around the board.

4. Castle early

In the centre, the king is exposed: files open up as pawns are exchanged, and enemy rooks and queens can attack it directly. Castling solves two problems at once. It tucks the king behind a wall of pawns on the side of the board, and it brings a rook towards the centre where it can join the game. Most strong players castle within the first ten moves.

After castling, be careful about moving the pawns in front of your king. Each pawn move creates a weakness that the opponent may later use for an attack.

5. Connect your rooks and make a plan

The opening is finished when your minor pieces are developed, your king is safe and your two rooks can see each other along the back rank ("connected rooks"). At that point, look for a plan: which side of the board is your position stronger on? Is there an open file where a rook could become active? Is there a weak square in the opponent's camp?

Three openings worth trying

  • The Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) – White develops quickly and aims the bishop at f7, the weakest point near Black's king. It is a classic way to learn open, tactical chess.
  • The Queen's Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4) – White offers a pawn to gain central control. In most lines Black cannot keep the extra pawn without problems, so the "gambit" is not really a sacrifice.
  • The London System (1.d4, Bf4, e3, Nf3, c3) – a solid set-up that you can play against almost anything. It is popular with club players because the plan is easy to remember.

Common opening mistakes

  • Moving too many pawns and leaving pieces at home.
  • Grabbing a pawn with the queen and falling behind in development.
  • Leaving a piece undefended – before every move, check which of your pieces are protected.
  • Ignoring the opponent's threat. The question "what does my opponent want?" will save more games than any opening book.

Practise with the computer

A good way to train these ideas is to play the same opening several times against the easy and medium computer levels in AG Classic Games. After each game, look back at the first ten moves: did you control the centre, develop every piece, and castle? You will soon find that your games start better – and that the middle game becomes much easier as a result.
