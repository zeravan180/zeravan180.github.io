▦ Dots and boxes strategy: the chain rule explained

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

Dots and boxes is a pencil-and-paper game that many of us played at school. Players take turns drawing a line between two neighbouring dots; whoever completes the fourth side of a box claims it and must draw another line. The player with the most boxes at the end wins. Behind this simple game lies some beautiful strategy.

The beginning: avoid the third side

At the start, the board is open and every line is safe as long as it does not create a box with three sides – because then the opponent can simply close it. Beginners usually play this way without thinking. The real game begins when there are no "safe" lines left and someone must give away boxes.

Chains and loops

By then, the board is divided into chains – rows of boxes connected so that taking one gives you the next – and loops, which are closed chains. When a player opens a chain, the opponent can take every box in it, one after another.

Giving away a short chain (one or two boxes) is cheap; giving away a long chain is expensive. Much of the strategy is about who will be forced to open the long chains.

The double-cross

Here is the key trick. When your opponent opens a long chain, you do not have to take all of it. You can take all boxes except the last two, and then draw the line that leaves those two boxes for your opponent as a pair (a "double-cross"). Your opponent takes the two boxes – but then must move again, and must open the next chain for you.

By giving up two boxes, you keep control: every time a new chain is opened, you take most of it and hand back only two. With loops, you give back four instead of two. Over several long chains, this wins easily.

The long chain rule

Elwyn Berlekamp, a mathematician who wrote a famous book about the game, described the long chain rule. In simple terms: the player who can make the number of long chains (three or more boxes) come out right at the end will be the one in control. On a board with an even number of dots, the first player wants an even number of long chains; with an odd number of dots, an odd number. The exact rule needs care with loops and short chains, but the idea is that the fight in the middle game is really a fight over how many long chains there will be.

Practical tips

  • In the opening, keep track of how many boxes have two sides – they are the ones that will become dangerous.
  • When the end approaches, count the long chains and think about who will have to open the first one.
  • Give away short chains first, and keep long chains for later.
  • When you are in control, remember the double-cross: take all but two.

Play on a big board

In AG Classic Games, dots and boxes is played on a large 15×10 board, against the computer or online. Large boards have many chains, so the strategy above makes a real difference – try counting chains in your next game.
