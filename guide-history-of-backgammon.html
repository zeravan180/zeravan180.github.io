📜 The long history of backgammon and tawla

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

Few games have a history as long as the family of race games that includes backgammon. Its roots go back thousands of years in the land between the rivers and its neighbours.

Ancient race games

People in the ancient Middle East played many games in which pieces race along a track according to the throw of dice. One of the most famous is the Royal Game of Ur, found in royal tombs in southern Mesopotamia (today's Iraq) and dated to around 2600–2400 BC. Its rules were partly reconstructed from a much later clay tablet written in cuneiform. It is not backgammon, but it shows how old the idea of a dice race game is in our region.

Archaeologists have also found game boards and dice from ancient Iran, Egypt and the Indus valley. Some writers connect an early board found at Shahr-e Sukhteh in eastern Iran with backgammon-like games, although scholars still debate how it was played.

Nard and the legend of Bozorgmehr

In Persia, a game called nard (or nardshir) was played on a board with two halves and twelve points on each side, very close to modern backgammon. A well-known Persian story, told in the Middle Persian text Wizārišn ī Čatrang and later in Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, describes how an Indian king sent chess to the Persian court as a challenge, and the wise minister Bozorgmehr answered by inventing nard. The board was said to represent the year and the days, and the throw of the dice the turning of fate. It is a legend, but it shows how highly the game was valued.

Tabula in Rome

The Romans played tabula, a game with fifteen pieces per player, three dice and a board of 24 points. It is widely seen as a close ancestor of backgammon. Versions of it spread through the Roman and Byzantine worlds.

Tawla, tavla and backgammon

Across the Middle East, the Balkans and Turkey, the game is still known by names that come from tabula or from nard: tawla in Arabic, tavla in Turkish, tavli in Greek and nardy in Russian. In English it has been called "backgammon" since the seventeenth century. The modern doubling cube was added in the United States in the 1920s, and international tournaments followed.

A café tradition

In Kurdistan and Iraq, tawla is part of everyday life. The click of the checkers and the rattle of the dice are familiar sounds in cafés and family gatherings, and players often call out the dice in Persian numbers – a reminder of the game's long journey. Whether you play on a wooden board or on your phone, you are playing one of humanity's oldest games.
