⚫ Dama (Turkish draughts): strategy for beginners

By the AG Classic Games team · Updated 9 November 2026

Dama, also called Turkish draughts, is played across Turkey, the Middle East and Kurdistan. It looks like checkers, but the pieces move forward and sideways instead of diagonally, and that changes the whole game.

A quick reminder of the rules

  • Each player has 16 pieces on an 8×8 board, placed on the second and third rows.
  • Ordinary pieces move one square forward or sideways, never backwards or diagonally.
  • Capturing is done by jumping over an enemy piece forwards or sideways to the empty square behind it, and several captures can be chained.
  • Capturing is compulsory, and you must choose the sequence that captures the most pieces.
  • A piece that reaches the last row becomes a king (dama), which moves any distance along rows and columns, like a rook in chess, and can capture from a distance.

1. Compulsory capture is a weapon

Because you must capture, your opponent can force your pieces to move – and you can do the same to them. The most common trick in dama is to offer one piece so that the opponent's forced capture lands their piece on a square where you can take two or three in return. Before every move, ask: if I place a piece here, what capture will my opponent be forced to make, and what happens after it?

2. Keep your pieces together

Pieces standing side by side or in short columns protect each other: an enemy piece cannot jump into an occupied square. Isolated pieces, on the other hand, can be attacked easily. Advance in groups, like a wall, rather than sending single pieces forward.

3. Guard your back row

Your last row is the opponent's promotion row. Keeping a few pieces there for as long as possible makes it hard for them to get a king. Many beginners move all their back pieces early and later find enemy pieces running through to promote.

4. The king changes everything

A king in dama is extremely strong: it can cross the whole board in one move and capture distant pieces. Getting the first king often decides the game. At the same time, the opponent's king can be dangerous even when you have more pieces, so plan your advance so that no enemy piece can slip through.

5. Count before you trade

Exchanges are frequent in dama. When you are ahead, trading pieces brings you closer to a winning ending; when you are behind, avoid simple trades and look for tactical chances. In the endgame, one king against one king is usually a draw, so plan to keep at least one extra piece.

Practise against the computer

Start against the easy computer in AG Classic Games and pay attention to every forced capture. After each game, try to find the moment when a capture was forced on you – or when you could have forced one on your opponent. That is where most dama games are won and lost.
